Introduction to the Problem
The Energy Challenge

Global electricity demand is increasing at an unprecedented rate, driven by artificial intelligence, expanding data centres, digital infrastructure, electric vehicles, and the electrification of industry. At the same time, nations must rapidly decarbonise their energy systems to meet the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals and achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. These challenges require a massive expansion of reliable, efficient, and low-carbon electricity generation.

(Show an animated globe with growing electricity demand, AI data centres, EVs, and renewable energy icons.)

The Role of Solar Energy

Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy has become one of the fastest-growing renewable energy technologies. Global solar generation has increased dramatically over the past decade and continues to expand as countries transition away from fossil fuels. As deployment accelerates, maintaining the reliability and availability of PV systems becomes increasingly important.

(Camera flies over the solar farm while panels illuminate.)

The Hidden Challenge

Although solar panels receive most of the attention, one of the most critical components of a photovoltaic system is the string inverter. The inverter converts the DC electricity produced by solar panels into AC electricity that can be supplied to the electrical grid.

(Highlight one inverter in the solar farm while the rest fade slightly.)

Why Inverters Matter

Modern PV systems may operate for more than 25 years, but inverter failures remain one of the leading causes of maintenance interventions and energy losses. Thermal stress, ageing, and degradation of power electronic components can reduce efficiency, shorten operational life, and increase maintenance costs.

(Animate entering the selected inverter.)

Thermal Interface Degradation

One of the key failure mechanisms occurs within the power electronics. As the thermal interface material between the IGBT power modules and the heat sink degrades, heat is no longer transferred efficiently. Localised hotspots develop, component temperatures rise, and the risk of failure increases.

(Show a simplified animation: IGBT → TIM → Heat Sink, with the TIM gradually degrading and a hotspot forming.)

Why Monitor Temperature?

Detecting thermal degradation at an early stage enables predictive maintenance before catastrophic failures occur. However, conventional temperature sensors only measure a few discrete locations and may fail to identify localised hotspots within the inverter.

(Compare a few point sensors against a hotspot that lies between them.)

Raman Distributed Temperature Sensing

Raman Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS) overcomes this limitation by measuring temperature continuously along an optical fibre. Instead of monitoring a handful of points, the fibre provides a distributed temperature profile capable of identifying the exact location of developing hotspots.

(Animate a pulse travelling through the fibre, followed by a coloured temperature profile appearing along its length.)

Digital Twin

This digital twin combines thermal modelling, Raman DTS measurements, and predictive analytics to provide an interactive representation of the inverter. Users can inspect components, visualise thermal behaviour, explore fault progression, and evaluate maintenance decisions in real time.
